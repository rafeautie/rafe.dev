The case was smaller than Ellis expected. He had pictured something long and grey with stencilled warnings, the kind of thing two people carry. Instead the clerk at the Ceres freight office slid across a box the size of a lunch tin, dark blue, warm to the touch, with a seal on the latch that blinked green when he picked it up.

"It knows you now," the clerk said. "Don't open it. Don't let it get cold. Don't let anyone else hold it for more than a minute."

"What happens after a minute?"

The clerk looked at him the way people look at someone who has asked how a parachute works on the way down. "It tells Thule," she said, and went back to her screen.

So that was the job. Two hundred and ninety days out to the edge of everything, one blue box, no questions. The money would clear his debt with eleven thousand to spare. He had done the sum on the back of his hand in the waiting room, twice, as though it might come out different.

---

The Kestrel was docked on the outer ring, between an ore hauler the size of a cathedral and a racing yacht someone had painted the color of a lime. She looked like what she was: a twenty-year-old courier ship with one bunk, one galley, and a reputation for getting there. Somebody had scratched a small bird into the paint beside the airlock. Ellis touched it on the way in, which he had not planned to do.

"Welcome aboard," said the ship. "You are Ellis Ward. You are four minutes late. Departure window closes in nineteen."

"Good morning to you too."

"It is 02:40 station time."

"Then good night." He strapped the case into the locker beside the bunk, where a green light came on to say the locker and the case were now on speaking terms. "Do you have a name?"

"My registration is KS-7741."

"I'm not calling you that for ten months."

A pause, which Ellis would later learn meant the ship was deciding whether to be offended. "The previous pilot called me Pell."

"Why Pell?"

"She did not say."

"Pell it is."

---

Leaving Ceres was less dramatic than he had hoped. The docking clamps let go with a sound like a large book closing. The ring slid away beneath them, then the dwarf planet itself, grey and pocked and lit at the edges, and then it was simply a bright star behind them, and then he could not tell which star it was.

He ate a packet of something labelled _Lentil Hearty_ and sat in the pilot's chair, watching nothing happen at enormous speed. This was the part people warned you about. Not danger, they said. The quiet. You find out what you brought with you.

What Ellis had brought, mostly, was his brother. Not in any useful sense. Just Danny, in the corner of every thought, saying the things Danny used to say. _You'll hate it. You'll be bored out of your mind by Tuesday._ It had been a year. People kept telling him it would get easier, and it had, a little, the way a stone gets smoother: not because anything is fixed, but because you keep turning it over.

"Pell," he said, "what do people usually do out here?"

"The previous pilot learned the cello."

"Was she any good?"

"No."

He laughed, properly, for the first time in a while.

---

He was nearly asleep when the console chimed.

It was not an alarm. It was the soft two-note tone the ship used for things that were interesting rather than urgent, and Ellis lay in the bunk for a moment hoping it would go away on its own.

"Pell?"

"Receiving a transmission. Very weak. Narrow band."

"From Ceres?"

"No. From ahead of us."

He sat up. On the screen a thin green line trembled, then rose in a short sequence of pulses, too regular to be noise and too simple to be speech. Seven pulses. A gap. Three more. Then nothing.

"Can you tell what it is?"

"No. It does not match any beacon, ship or station on record." Another of Pell's pauses. "It has repeated four times since I first detected it. The interval is ninety-one minutes exactly."

Ellis looked at the line, waiting. Across the cabin, in its locker, the blue case sat in the dark. He could have sworn its little light was blinking slightly faster than before.

He told himself he was tired. He told himself space was full of strange echoes, and that a courier who started seeing meaning in every blip would be a wreck by the asteroid belt.

Then he set an alarm for ninety-one minutes, and lay awake waiting for it.
